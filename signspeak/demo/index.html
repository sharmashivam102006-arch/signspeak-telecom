Live captions

Captions appear here. Start the camera, teach a few signs, then sign them.

MediaPipe weights (~12 MB) download on first start. Everything after that runs offline on your machine — no frame and no hand position ever leaves this browser.

Pick a sign, hold Space or press Record, perform it once, stop. Aim for 8–12 takes each, and vary them on purpose: different distance from the camera, slightly different speed, hands in a slightly different place. Ten identical takes teach the model that the sign means exactly that, and it will then fail on the eleventh.

A NONE class is pre-listed and it is not optional. Record yourself scratching your nose, waving, reaching off-screen and gesturing while talking. Without it the classifier is closed-set: it must pick one of your signs, so a stranger's idle movement gets announced out loud as a word. This is the single biggest difference between a demo that survives contact with a real person and one that does not.

Direct browser-to-browser call with no server at all. One side creates an offer, the other pastes it back — copy the text between the two machines any way you like. Works on the same network without extra setup; across the internet it needs a TURN server, which is exactly the problem LiveKit solves in the full app.

    Watch which term dominates. It is almost never the classifier — it is the detection lag, the stillness the segmenter waits for before deciding the sign is over. Speeding up the model does nothing for perceived latency; shortening that wait does, at the cost of chopping signs in half. That trade-off is the interesting engineering result in this project, and it is worth a section in the report.

    Thresholds

    Drag the last one and watch the latency number move. Lower is snappier and starts cutting slow signs in two; higher is patient and feels sluggish. There is no correct value — there is a value you choose for an error budget, which is what calibrate.py --fit-thresholds does properly in the full project.
